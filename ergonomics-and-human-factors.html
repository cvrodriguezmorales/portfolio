Ergonomics and Human Factors

C. Rodríguez Morales July 12, 2026
Ergonomics and Human Factors taught me that whether analyzing an assembly line or designing a new control interface, my goal should be to adapt the work to the worker. Here is a look at the methodologies and principles I used to make that happen.

The Physiology and Biomechanics of Work: Before designing a task, we must understand the physical limits of the human body. By measuring maximum oxygen consumption ($VO_2\text{Max}$) and analyzing heart rate reserves (utilizing the formula $HR_{\text{Max}} = 220 - \text{Edad}$), we can determine a worker's physical capacity to perform sustained tasks without risking overexertion. Understanding spinal biomechanics allows us to classify physical workloads and design tasks that prevent the chronic lower back pain that plagues so many industrial workers.


Quantifying Ergonomic Risk: Guesswork is not an option when worker safety is on the line. I learned to apply rigorous quantitative tools to evaluate manual material handling:


  • The NIOSH Lifting Equation: By calculating the Recommended Weight Limit ($RWL$) for two-handed manual lifting tasks, we establish the maximum safe weight for a specific environment. Calculating the Lifting Index ($LI$) allows us to immediately flag high-risk tasks that require structural redesigns.
  • Snook-Ciriello Tables: For tasks involving pushing, pulling, or carrying, we utilize these psychophysical tables (developed by Liberty Mutual) to ensure that the required forces remain acceptable for the vast majority of the workforce.

Bridging Mind and Machine (Human Factors): Physical safety is only half the equation. Human error accounts for 70% to 90% of industrial accidents, and these errors are often the direct result of poor design. Through Cognitive Engineering, I learned to design control panels and interfaces that align with natural human expectations—also known as population stereotypes.


By applying concepts like Warrick's Principle and ensuring strong stimulus-response compatibility, we can build systems where the right action feels intuitive. Furthermore, designing the physical resistance of controls (such as elastic, static sliding, or viscous resistance) provides tactile feedback that reduces cognitive load and prevents costly mistakes.

Controlling the Environment and Hazards:

A safe workstation extends to the surrounding environment. I gained the skills to design comprehensive Industrial Hygiene controls, including:

  • Acoustic Management: Evaluating noise exposure against OSHA's 90 dB Permissible Exposure Limit (PEL) using dosimeters and sound level meters, and implementing Hearing Conservation Programs when necessary.
  • Lighting Optimization: Balancing illuminance (measured in footcandles or lux) and minimizing direct and indirect glare to ensure workers can perform precision tasks safely and comfortably.

Implementing Real Solutions: Ultimately, these analyses culminate in the application of the Hierarchy of Controls. The goal is always to eliminate or substitute hazards first. When that isn't possible, we rely on engineering controls to isolate the worker from the risk. For example, this includes selecting hand tools that weigh under 3 pounds for continuous use, utilizing bent-handle designs to maintain neutral wrist postures, and deploying anti-vibration technology to prevent musculoskeletal disorders.
