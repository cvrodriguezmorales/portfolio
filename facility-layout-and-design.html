Facility Layout and Design

C. Rodríguez Morales November 6, 2026

A factory is more than the machines inside it. Facilities Layout and Design, which I'm taking in the fall of 2026, is teaching me to think about a factory as a whole: where every department goes, how material moves between them, and how a building has to change when a company grows or adds a new product. Instead of designing a single workstation, I am learning to design the facility itself, starting from a clear definition of the problem and building up, step by step, the evidence needed to justify a layout.

  • The technical skills gained: 
    • Facilities Design as Engineering Design - Applying the engineering design process to facilities, and distinguishing a facility, its layout, the facility design, and the overall facilities plan.
    • Defining the Design Problem - Turning a business need into a design basis with a formal problem statement, objectives, scope and system boundary, requirements, constraints, and design criteria before proposing any layout.
    • PQRST Analysis - Gathering and interpreting the Product, Quantity, Routing, Supporting services, and Timing information a layout depends on, and translating each piece into facility requirements.
    • Material Flow Analysis - Converting production demand into physical movements and equivalent unit loads, and building from-to charts to measure the direction and intensity of flow between activities.
    • Activity Relationship Analysis - Assigning closeness ratings (A, E, I, O, U, X) backed by evidence and reason codes, converting flow intensity into closeness letters, and building Activity Relationship Charts and Diagrams with Muther's notation.
    • Computer-Aided Layout Design - Modeling facilities and workstations in 3D with SketchUp, drafting floor plans to scale in AutoCAD from real measurements, and drawing relationship diagrams in yEd.

Course Case Study: Caribe Surgical Access

 Team semester project — ININ 4040: Facilities Layout and Design (in progress)

The problem: Caribe Surgical Access (CSA) is a medical-device manufacturer in Mayagüez, Puerto Rico, that makes two single-use surgical trocars, T5 and T10, devices used to create and maintain a small opening through the abdominal wall during minimally invasive surgery. The plant molds its own plastic components, machines stainless-steel shafts, and assembles, tests, and packages each device in a 26,400 ft² building. CSA now needs to introduce a third product, T12, and grow from 192,000 to 266,400 good devices per year in Year 1 (about 39% more), reaching 325,000 by Year 5. All of this has to happen on a single shift, inside the existing building, and without moving its walls, columns, docks, or emergency exits. Our team was hired to determine what must change in the facility to handle this growth and the new product.

Our approach: Before proposing any layout, we built the design basis. We defined the problem formally, including the system boundary (from the receiving docks on the west to the shipping docks on the east), the stakeholders, the requirements, and the fixed constraints, and calculated the required pace: a takt time of 23.6 seconds per unit in Year 1 and 19.4 seconds by Year 5. We then documented the full PQRST information: the 18 part numbers and which ones are made in-house or bought, the routes of the five material streams through the plant, demand and product mix by year, and the supporting services. From there, we identified 28 facility activities and rated how close each pair should be using Muther's closeness codes. Every data point and assumption was logged in an evidence register of 45 entries, each marked as current, assumed, or still to be verified.

What we found so far:

  • Capacity is the biggest risk. A rough load check puts cannula subassembly at about 97% in Year 1 and 120% in Year 5, and the Swiss CNC lathes at about 95% and 119%, leaving little or no margin.
  • The product mix makes it harder. Demand shifts toward T12, which has the longest process times and the lowest first-pass test conformance (94.5%), so the workload grows faster than the number of units.
  • Material flow is inefficient. Each device crosses the main aisle twice and backtracks between assembly, testing, and packaging, and that 10 ft aisle carries all five material streams, mixing forklifts, pallet jacks, and carts right next to the controlled assembly room.
  • There is an opportunity. The three products share most of their architecture, T12 only adds three unique parts, and there is underused space in the building plus an 8,800 ft² expansion reserve on the north side.

Next steps: The next stage of the project is a detailed capacity calculation for each resource that includes changeovers and allowances, a from-to chart of flow intensity between activities, an activity relationship diagram, and space requirements for Years 1 and 5, which together will lead to our proposed layout.

Skills applied: Facility design problem definition, PQRST analysis, takt time, routing and material flow analysis, activity modeling, Muther's closeness ratings, capacity load analysis, evidence and assumptions register, teamwork and technical writing
