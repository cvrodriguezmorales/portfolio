Applied Industrial Statistics

C. Rodríguez Morales July 12, 2026

I took this course in the fall of 2025, and it taught me the main statistical concepts of Industrial Engineering, the ones that made me fall in love with what IE is. During this course, I learned the theoretical and applied background needed to develop and effectively use statistical and probabilistic models for decision making in engineering.

  • The technical skills gained: 
    • Regression Analysis - Building and validating statistical models to predict outcomes, including handling multicollinearity.
    • Design of Experiments - Testing whether or not different conditions/treatments actually produce different results.
    • Goodness-of-fit testing: Assessing if real-world data matches a theoretical distribution to understand its behavior. 

Final Course Project: Regression Analysis for Utility Planning

 Team project — ININ 4020: Applied Industrial Statistics

The problem: A utility company (Lux Inc.) wanted to better understand what drives daily energy consumption across the buildings it serves, with the goal of offering personalized efficiency recommendations and improving resource allocation. My team and I were given a dataset of 80 buildings, including square footage, occupancy, appliance load, average temperature, building type, and day of the week, and tasked with building a model that could reliably predict energy usage from these characteristics.

Our approach: Rather than jumping straight to a "final" model, we built it in stages, starting simple and adding complexity only when it was justified by the data. We began with a baseline model using just the physical characteristics of each building, then progressively layered in environmental effects (temperature), behavioral patterns (weekday vs. weekend usage), and interaction effects between variables, testing at each step whether the added complexity was actually earning its keep.

We validated each model using ANOVA, t-tests for individual predictors, and residual diagnostics (normal probability plots, versus-fits plots) to check that our assumptions held up. We used variance inflation factors (VIF) to make sure our predictors weren't dangerously correlated with each other. All analysis was done in Minitab.

Results:

  • Square footage was the single strongest driver of energy use. Bigger buildings consistently use more energy, no surprise there.
  • Building type mattered a lot: industrial buildings carry a much higher baseline energy demand than commercial or residential buildings, regardless of size.
  • Each additional appliance added roughly 20 kWh to daily consumption.
  • Energy use reliably dropped on weekends, capturing real behavioral patterns in how buildings are used.
  • Temperature had a smaller, but still statistically significant, effect; colder days used more energy than warmer ones, suggesting heating costs outweigh cooling costs in this dataset.

The judgment call: Our most technically "perfect" model (Model 5) achieved a 100% R² and virtually zero prediction error; but it also included two interaction terms that didn't meaningfully improve accuracy in practice. We recommended a simpler model (Model 4) for Lux Inc.'s actual field use instead, applying Occam's Razor: if two models explain the data equally well, the simpler one is the better choice for real-world decision-making. 

Skills applied: Multiple linear regression, ANOVA, residual diagnostics, multicollinearity assessment (VIF), model selection, Minitab, data-driven business recommendations
