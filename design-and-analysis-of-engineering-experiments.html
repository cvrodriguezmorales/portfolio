Design and Analysis of Engineering Experiments

C. Rodríguez Morales July 12, 2026

Course Foundations & Skills Mastered

Rather than just learning statistical theories, this class provided a rigorous framework for identifying where variations happen and how to systematically tune a process to hit optimal targets. It taught me how to turn raw statistical concepts into actionable engineering decisions. Here is a breakdown of my core takeaways:

Skill AreaPractical Application
Statistical Analysis
Running hypothesis tests and calculating confidence intervals to accurately evaluate sampling errors in a given process.
Experimental Design
Setting up randomized blocks, Latin squares, and split plots to control nuisance factors and manage everyday variability.
Factorial Models
Building out two-level fractional and full factorial designs to figure out which factors actually matter and how they interact with each other.
Response Optimization
Using response surface methodology and Central Composite Designs (CCD) to measure curvature and find the mathematical "sweet spot" for a target.

Software & Analytical Tools

To bring these models to life and handle the heavy lifting of data analysis, I relied on a strong technical stack to design and validate our experiments:

  • Minitab: This was my go-to for executing ANOVA, generating Pareto charts, mapping out main effects, and running rigorous residual tests to make sure our models were actually adequate.

Real-World Application: Cart Acceleration Simulation

To put all these methodologies to the test, my team built an in-silico experiment simulating a gravity-driven cart system. The goal was to figure out the safest, most efficient way to transfer manufacturing materials between stations. We specifically looked at how the ramp angle, friction coefficient, and cart mass influenced the cart's acceleration.

  • We built a full factorial design (with center points) and completely randomized our run order to make sure the simulator's timing quirks wouldn't introduce measurement errors.
  • We calculated our response variables using the Successive Difference Method, governed by the equation a = [(x4 + x5 + x6) - (x1 + x2 + x3)] / 9t2.
  • The data proved that a steeper ramp naturally spiked acceleration, while friction pushed back hard as a direct opposing force—essentially letting us map out Newton's Second Law statistically.
  • After spotting some significant curvature in our initial data, we scaled up to a 40-run Central Composite Design. We ultimately found a lack of fit at the extreme limits, which was a great lesson in recognizing when a physical system is just too complex to be boxed into a standard quadratic model and requires non-linear regression instead.

Industrial Value & Process Optimization

Being able to statistically validate a system instead of relying on trial-and-error is everything in modern manufacturing. These Design of Experiments (DOE) methodologies directly translate to the hands-on challenges I tackle on the floor—whether that means using data to drive cycle time reductions, evaluating work content, or pushing for continuous improvement and process optimization on a vehicle assembly line.
